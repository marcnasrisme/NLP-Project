1. Your question, sharpened

You noticed: transformers process variable-length sequences just fine. Mistral happily takes 5 tokens or 500. So why does the gating head need a fixed-size input (and therefore mean pooling)? Couldn't we just feed the same variable-length representation the transformer uses?

The answer is yes-and-no, and unpacking it forces us to look at how transformers handle variable length, which is exactly what self-attention does well. After that we can talk about layers.

2. The shape problem: variable length vs fixed-size head

The gating head is an MLP. Its first layer is a matrix multiply $W_1 \bar h$, with $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times 4096}$. For matrix multiplication to work, the input $\bar h$ has to be a vector of exactly 4096 numbers — not a matrix of $T \times 4096$ where $T$ varies.

Mistral, in contrast, processes any $T$ because its core operation isn't a single big matrix multiply against a fixed-shape parameter — it's self-attention, which is shape-polymorphic. Let's see why.

3. How a transformer actually works

3.1 Tokens and embeddings

Step one: text becomes integers (token IDs).

"I just got promoted!" │ ▼ tokenizer [ 1, 314, 1444, 4738, 13821, 28808 ] ← T = 6 token IDs │ ▼ embedding lookup (table E ∈ R^{V × d}, V = vocab size, d = 4096) h⁰ ∈ R^{6 × 4096} ← T rows of d-dimensional vectors

The embedding is a learned lookup: each token ID indexes a row of an embedding matrix $E$, giving a $d=4096$-dimensional vector. Stacking $T$ such vectors gives a $T \times d$ matrix $h^{0}$. The superscript means "after layer 0," i.e., before any transformer block.

Notice: $T$ can be anything. The embedding table doesn't care how many lookups you do.

(Position information is added on top — Mistral uses Rotary Position Embeddings, RoPE, baked into the attention computation. Skip the details.)

3.2 Self-attention — the magic ingredient

Self-attention takes a $T \times d$ matrix $h$ and produces another $T \times d$ matrix where each row is a context-aware mix of all the input rows.

Mechanically, three projections (these are the LoRA targets!):

$$ Q = h W_Q, \quad K = h W_K, \quad V = h W_V, \qquad W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}. $$

So $Q, K, V$ are each $T \times d$. Then attention is computed as:

$$ \mathrm{Attn}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d}} \right) V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}. $$

What does this do?

  • $Q K^\top$ is a $T \times T$ matrix. Entry $(i, j)$ measures how much token $i$ should attend to token $j$ (a dot product of token $i$'s "query" with token $j$'s "key").
  • The softmax normalizes each row to a probability distribution over the $T$ positions. Row $i$: "of all positions, how much weight do I put on each when summarizing my context?"
  • Multiplying by $V$ produces a weighted sum of the value vectors — the output at position $i$ is a context-aware blend of all positions.

Tiny worked example, $T=3, d=2$

Suppose $Q = K = V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (3 tokens, 2 dims; just for arithmetic).

$$ QK^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Apply softmax row-wise (skipping the $\sqrt{d}$ for clarity):

$$ A = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top) \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.42 & 0.16 & 0.42 \\ 0.16 & 0.42 & 0.42 \\ 0.21 & 0.21 & 0.58 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Each row sums to 1 — it's a distribution. Then $A V$ produces a $T \times d$ output where each row is $A_i \cdot V$ — a weighted average of $V$'s rows.

Token 3 (last row of $A$) gives 58% weight to itself and 21% each to tokens 1 and 2. That's the "context aggregation" baked into a single layer.

3.3 Why attention handles any sequence length

Look at the shapes:

ObjectShapeDepends on $T$?
$h$$T \times d$yes (varies)
$W_Q, W_K, W_V$$d \times d$no (fixed)
$Q, K, V$$T \times d$yes
$QK^\top$$T \times T$yes
output$T \times d$yes

The parameters are all $d \times d$ — fixed shape. Only the activations grow with $T$. The matrix multiplications still work no matter what $T$ is, because matrix multiplication broadcasts over the sequence dimension.

Compare with our gating MLP:

ObjectShape
$W_1$$1024 \times 4096$
required input$\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ — exactly one vector

The MLP could be applied to each row of the $T \times 4096$ matrix independently — that would produce a $T \times 4$ output. But our supervision signal is one cluster label per conversation, not per token. We need a single 4-vector for the whole conversation. Hence the pooling step.

The clean way to phrase it: the transformer outputs a representation per token (variable length). We want a representation per conversation (fixed = one). Pooling is the bridge from many to one.

3.4 The full transformer block

One transformer "block" or "layer" does this:

input h^{ℓ-1} (shape T × d) │ ▼ ┌─────────────────────────────┐ │ LayerNorm │ │ │ │ │ ▼ │ │ Multi-head Self-Attention │ ← uses W_Q^ℓ, W_K^ℓ, W_V^ℓ, W_O^ℓ │ │ │ │ ▼ │ │ + (residual) ◄── h^{ℓ-1} │ │ │ │ │ ▼ │ │ LayerNorm │ │ │ │ │ ▼ │ │ Feed-Forward (MLP) │ ← uses W_up^ℓ, W_down^ℓ │ │ │ │ ▼ │ │ + (residual) │ └─────────────────────────────┘ │ ▼ output h^ℓ (shape T × d)

Two sub-modules: self-attention and a feed-forward network (a 2-layer MLP applied per-token). Residual connections (the "+" arrows) help gradients flow. Layer normalization stabilizes training.

The crucial point for what comes next: the same block structure repeats $L$ times — Mistral-7B has $L = 32$ layers. Each layer has its own copy of the parameters $W_Q^\ell, W_K^\ell, W_V^\ell, W_O^\ell$ and the FFN weights. The hidden state $h^\ell$ evolves layer by layer.

4. Pooling alternatives the gate could use

Now that we've seen the transformer's output is $h \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$, the question becomes: which single vector do we feed into the gate? There's no need to mean-pool — that's just one choice. Here are the common options:

MethodFormulaProsCons
Mean pool (current) $\bar h = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t h_t$ simple, robust to position dilutes localized signals (e.g. emotional cue tokens get washed out)
Last-token $\bar h = h_T$ in causal LMs, $h_T$ has seen everything; matches generation conditioning relies on model concentrating info at the end
First-token / CLS $\bar h = h_1$ standard for BERT-style classification causal Mistral has no CLS; first token sees nothing
Attention pool $\bar h = \sum_t a_t h_t$, $a_t$ learned focuses on the informative tokens more parameters; another thing that can collapse
Max pool $\bar h_d = \max_t h_{t,d}$ preserves peaks discards everything but the max
Your DESA gate uses mean pool. Given how the gate has collapsed (see previous explainer §9), switching to last-token or attention pool is one of the highest-leverage fixes. The emotional content of "I just got fired and I don't know what to do" lives at specific words, not in the average.

5. The "use the last token" shortcut — a powerful detail

Mistral is a causal language model: token $t$ only attends to tokens $1, \dots, t$, never to the future. A direct consequence: $h_T$ — the hidden state at the final position — has aggregated information from every previous token through self-attention.

So in a causal LM, last-token pooling is essentially free summary aggregation:

$$ \bar h_{\text{last}} = h_T \quad \in \mathbb{R}^{4096}, \quad \text{contains context about all of } 1..T. $$

This is why instruction-tuned causal LMs are often used as embedders by just grabbing the last hidden state. It often beats mean pooling, especially for variable-length text.

Both approaches solve the same shape problem (turn $T \times d$ into $d$). They differ in where the summary signal lives.

6. What "by layer" means

Recall: Mistral-7B is a stack of 32 transformer blocks, indexed $\ell = 1, \dots, 32$. The hidden state evolves as you go up the stack:

top of model layer 32 h³² ──── output projection ──── token logits → next-word prediction ▲ │ layer 31 h³¹ ▲ │ ⋮ ▲ │ layer 2 h² ▲ │ layer 1 h¹ ▲ │ layer 0 h⁰ = embedding(tokens) bottom of model

What does each layer "do"? It's not perfectly understood, but a robust empirical pattern across many transformer studies:

DepthTends to encode
Bottom layers (1–8 of 32)Token-level / surface features: morphology, syntax, local patterns, "what word is this".
Middle layers (~9–22)Sentence structure, grammatical relations, coreference.
Top layers (~23–32)Task-relevant semantics, abstract meaning, "what should come next".

Concretely, at each layer the model has:

$$ h^\ell = h^{\ell-1} + \text{Attn}^\ell(\text{LN}(h^{\ell-1})) + \text{FFN}^\ell(\text{LN}(\cdot)) $$

where $\text{Attn}^\ell$ uses its own $W_Q^\ell, W_K^\ell, W_V^\ell, W_O^\ell$ matrices. Each layer has its own ~64M parameters; in total, the 32 layers contain most of Mistral's 7B parameters.

Key fact: "layer" doesn't mean "step in time" or "step through the sequence." It means depth in the network. All $T$ tokens go through layer 1, then all $T$ tokens go through layer 2, and so on. The matrix $h^\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$ exists at every depth $\ell$.

7. LoRA lives at every layer

Now back to LoRA. The config in configs/qlora_config.yaml says:

target_modules:
  - "q_proj"   # the W_Q matrix
  - "k_proj"   # the W_K matrix
  - "v_proj"   # the W_V matrix
  - "o_proj"   # the output projection W_O after attention

For each transformer block, LoRA inserts an adapter on each of these four matrices. Since there are 32 blocks, that means each cluster expert has:

$$ 4 \text{ matrices} \times 32 \text{ layers} = 128 \text{ LoRA adapter pairs } (A, B) \text{ per cluster.} $$

Each $(A, B)$ has $r \cdot d + d \cdot r = 2dr$ parameters. With $d = 4096, r = 16$:

$$ 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 16 = 131{,}072 \text{ params per adapter pair.} $$

Times 128: about 16.8M trainable params per cluster expert. Times 4 clusters: ~67M trainable params total — about 1% of Mistral's 7B.

The point: when we say "the cluster-0 LoRA expert," we don't mean a single adapter — we mean 128 small adapters sprinkled throughout the network, one at each $(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O)$ at each of the 32 layers.

cluster-0 expert cluster-1 expert ... cluster-3 expert ──────────────── ──────────────── ──────────────── layer 32 q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs layer 31 q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ layer 2 q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs layer 1 q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs q,k,v,o LoRA pairs 4 × 32 = 128 pairs 128 pairs 128 pairs

For the turn-level gate, all 128 adapter pairs in a given expert are turned on with the same weight $\alpha_k$. So if $\alpha_0 = 0.6$, every one of cluster-0's 128 adapter pairs is scaled by 0.6 throughout the network. One scalar per expert; that scalar is reused at every layer.

8. X-LoRA decoded: token × layer × adapter

X-LoRA's whole pitch: route differently at every depth and every position.

Let's be precise about the routing tensor's shape. With $K = 4$ adapters, $L = 32$ layers, $T$ tokens (variable):

$$ g \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times L \times K}, \quad \text{with } \sum_{k=1}^{K} g_{t, \ell, k} = 1 \text{ for every } (t, \ell). $$

That is: at every (token position, layer) pair, X-LoRA produces its own probability distribution over the 4 cluster experts. For one input of length $T = 50$, that's $50 \times 32 = 1600$ separate softmax distributions, all coming out of one classifier.

How the LoRA update is applied at one layer

At layer $\ell$, for the attention $W_Q^\ell$ matrix, the effective weight applied to token $t$ is:

$$ W_{Q,\text{eff}}^{\ell, t} = W_Q^\ell + \sum_{k=1}^{4} g_{t, \ell, k} \, B_Q^{(k, \ell)} A_Q^{(k, \ell)}. $$

So token 3's projection might be 80% cluster-2's adapter at layer 5, while token 4's projection at the same layer 5 might be 60% cluster-3's adapter. And the same token 3 at layer 6 might be 70% cluster-1's adapter.

This is much finer than the turn-level gate, which is just:

$$ W_{Q,\text{eff}}^{\ell} = W_Q^\ell + \sum_{k=1}^{4} \alpha_{k} \, B_Q^{(k, \ell)} A_Q^{(k, \ell)}, \quad \alpha \text{ does not depend on } t \text{ or } \ell. $$

The X-LoRA classifier — one head, three output dimensions

How does one small classifier produce a $(T, L, K)$ tensor? The X-LoRA architecture (per PEFT's implementation, configured in inference.py:104-115):

  1. First forward pass through Mistral with adapters disabled.
  2. Collect hidden states from every layer: $h^1, h^2, \ldots, h^{32}$, each of shape $T \times d$.
  3. The classifier (a small MLP with xlora_depth=4, xlora_size=2048) takes these stacked features and produces logits of shape $T \times L \times K$.
  4. Apply softmax across the $K$ dimension to get $g$.
  5. Second forward pass: at layer $\ell$, position $t$, use $g_{t,\ell,:}$ to weight the LoRA update.

Variable length is no problem here either — the classifier processes per-token, just like the transformer itself. Output and input share the same $T$.

What "layerwise_scalings=True" does

That config flag (inference.py:111) is what enables the per-layer dimension. With it off, the classifier produces just $(T, K)$ — same routing at every layer. With it on, you get the full $(T, L, K)$.

9. Why per-layer routing could matter

Recall the empirical pattern: lower layers do syntax-ish things, higher layers do semantics. A plausible story for emotional dialogue:

  • At low layers, all four cluster experts likely have similar adaptations (they all saw English conversational text). Routing here matters less.
  • At middle layers, where conversational pragmatics get encoded, the cluster experts diverge — "negative_high_arousal" learned to attend to complaint phrasing differently than "positive_low_arousal" learned to attend to gratitude.
  • At top layers, where output token prediction is being shaped, the experts disagree most about which words to produce.

The flexibility to weight experts differently at different depths means X-LoRA could express things like: "use the angry expert mostly at layers 18–22 where lexical choice happens, but use a uniform mix at layers 1–5 where it doesn't matter." A turn-level gate can't do that — it commits to one weighting for the whole stack.

However, in your run X-LoRA is collapsed to uniform at every $(t, \ell)$. The expressive power is wasted; the classifier never learned to make distinctions. See the previous explainer's §9.2 — likely cause is the classifier state never loaded properly, or the optimizer got stuck at the uniform fixed point.

Tying it back to your question

"Why don't we use the variable-length representation directly for the gate?" Three short answers:

  1. You can't multiply a $T \times d$ matrix by a fixed-shape weight matrix and get a single 4-vector. Something has to collapse the $T$ dimension. The transformer doesn't need to because attention is shape-polymorphic; an MLP head does need to.
  2. Mean pooling is one choice; better choices exist. Last-token pooling, attention pooling, or even feeding all $T$ tokens through the gate independently and averaging the per-token alphas afterward are all sensible alternatives — and any of them might revive your collapsed gate.
  3. X-LoRA does exactly the "use variable-length" idea, but for routing. It produces one routing distribution per token (and per layer), not just one per conversation. The cost is that the classifier is bigger and harder to train — and in your case it underfit to the uniform distribution.

The mean-pool vs per-token tradeoff is the same tradeoff as turn-level gate vs X-LoRA: simpler aggregation = easier to train, less expressive; finer aggregation = more expressive, harder to train without collapse.

End of follow-up. The "transformer" is a stack of 32 attention+FFN blocks; "layer" is depth in that stack; LoRA is sprinkled at every layer; X-LoRA can route differently at every depth and every token.
