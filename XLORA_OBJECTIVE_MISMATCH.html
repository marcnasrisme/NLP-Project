X-LoRA's Training-Objective Mismatch

Why pooling across all tokens during training contradicts what per-token routing is for, what we did instead, and the four ways out.

1. The question

You asked: "Why should we aggregate over all tokens for X-LoRA — isn't the whole point for each and every token to trigger a specific LoRA or blend of LoRAs?"

The answer is yes, that is the point. And our training objective doesn't honor it. Your intuition spotted a real, structural problem.

2. The mismatch in one sentence

X-LoRA is designed to make $T \times L \times K$ routing decisions per example. We are supervising it with one label per example. The gradient signal is too coarse for the architecture's expressivity.

Concretely, with $T \approx 80$ tokens and $L = 32$ layers and $K = 4$ adapters, each forward pass produces $80 \times 32 \times 4 \approx 10{,}000$ routing scalings. Our training collapses all of that down to a single 4-class supervised target.

3. The math: why pooled CE produces diffuse routing

The objective we're using is the pooled-logit cross-entropy:

$$ \bar z = \frac{1}{T_{\text{real}} \cdot L} \sum_{t, \ell} z_{t, \ell}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\log \sigma(\bar z)_y, $$

where $z_{t, \ell} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is the classifier's per-(token, layer) logit vector, $\sigma$ is softmax over the $K$ adapter dimension, and $y$ is the gold cluster index.

Now apply the chain rule to find the gradient hitting any single position's logit:

$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z_{t, \ell}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial \bar z}}_{\text{shape } K} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial \bar z}{\partial z_{t, \ell}}}_{= \frac{1}{T_{\text{real}} \cdot L}}. $$

So each individual $z_{t, \ell}$ receives a gradient scaled down by $1/(T_{\text{real}} \cdot L)$. For our typical input ($T_{\text{real}} \approx 80, L = 32$), that's a factor of

$$ \frac{1}{80 \cdot 32} = \frac{1}{2560} \approx 4 \times 10^{-4}. $$

The CE term essentially says: "the average across 2560 routing decisions should point at the gold cluster." Gradient descent finds the lazy way to satisfy that constraint: a tiny per-position bias toward gold, without any individual position being peaked.

This is exactly what we observed during training. The X-LoRA classifier reached epoch_acc = 0.579 on the pooled prediction — but per-(token, layer) entropy stayed at ~1.38 (essentially $\log 4$, i.e. uniform). The classifier learned the average, not the parts.

Numerical illustration

Suppose every $(t, \ell)$ outputs softmax probabilities $(0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.25)$ — a 5-percentage-point tilt toward cluster 0. Then:

  • The pooled prediction is also $(0.30, 0.25, 0.20, 0.25)$, $\arg\max = 0$. Pooled accuracy on cluster-0 examples: 100%.
  • Per-position entropy: $H = -(0.3\ln 0.3 + 0.25\ln 0.25 + 0.20\ln 0.20 + 0.25\ln 0.25) \approx 1.376$. Essentially uniform.

You can have a "correct" pooled prediction with completely diffuse per-position routing. The supervision lets it.

4. Why we did it anyway

EmpatheticDialogues gives us conversation-level labels — one emotion per dialogue. We do not have per-token labels saying "this token should route to the angry expert." So how do we translate one label into per-position supervision? You have to pick a compromise. The original notebook 03 picked option 1 below; we kept it.

The four options form a spectrum:

Option What it supervises Per-position routing Cost
1. Pooled CE (current) $\bar z = \mathrm{mean}_{t,\ell} z_{t,\ell}$ diffuse — only the average matters cheap
2. Per-(t, ℓ) CE $z_{t,\ell}$ for every $(t, \ell)$ peaked but homogeneous (every position aims at gold) cheap
3. Per-layer pooled CE $\bar z_\ell = \mathrm{mean}_t z_{t,\ell}$ for each $\ell$ peaked per layer, free per token within cheap
4. Next-token-prediction loss standard LM CE on the gold response emerges from optimization expensive (full graph through 32 layers)

5. The four alternatives, in detail

Option 1 — pooled CE (what we have)

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{pool}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\frac{1}{T_{\text{real}} L} \sum_{t,\ell} z_{t,\ell}\right)_{\!y} $$

Coarse but stable. Each per-position gradient is scaled by $1/(T_{\text{real}} L)$, so individual $(t, \ell)$ softmaxes never sharpen. Result we observed: pooled accuracy 0.58, per-position entropy ~1.38.

Option 2 — per-(t, ℓ) CE against the same label

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{per-pos}} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{real}} L} \sum_{t, \ell} -\log \sigma(z_{t, \ell})_y $$

Now each position gets a full-magnitude gradient — no $1/(T_{\text{real}} L)$ dampening — because it's its own CE term. Per-position softmaxes will sharpen rapidly and entropy will plummet.

The catch: you've imposed the strong assumption that every $(t, \ell)$ should route to the gold cluster. That's exactly the assumption X-LoRA was built to relax. The result would be peaked routing that's homogeneous across positions and layers — equivalent to a very over-confident turn-level gate replicated 2560 times. Sharp, but not discriminative.

Option 3 — per-layer pooled CE

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{per-layer}} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} -\log \sigma\!\left(\frac{1}{T_{\text{real}}} \sum_{t} z_{t, \ell}\right)_{\!y} $$

Pool over tokens only; supervise each layer's averaged decision separately. Each layer's per-token gradient is scaled by just $1/T_{\text{real}}$ — much stronger than option 1's $1/(T_{\text{real}} L)$. And different layers can specialize: layer 5's average can disagree with layer 25's.

This is the middle-ground compromise. Forces each layer's averaged routing to be correct, while letting per-token variation within a layer happen, and letting per-layer behavior diverge.

Option 4 — next-token-prediction loss with the X-LoRA stack live

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{NTP}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(y_i \mid y_{Don't supervise routing directly at all. Train the classifier by running full forward passes with X-LoRA's per-position routing live, computing standard LM cross-entropy on the gold response, and back-propagating through the X-LoRA stack to the classifier. The base model and the four LoRA experts stay frozen — only the classifier head (~16M params) updates.

Gradients reach the classifier through the 32 frozen transformer layers. PyTorch keeps the computational graph alive even when leaf parameters have requires_grad=False, so this works mechanically. The classifier learns which experts are useful at which positions because the routing affects the LM loss, and routing decisions emerge from optimization rather than being hand-supervised.

The intellectual difference: options 1–3 treat routing as the target ("predict the right cluster"). Option 4 treats routing as a means ("produce the right response"). Only option 4 lets the per-position pattern be data-driven.

Cost: VRAM for the activation graph through 32 transformer layers in FP16 — non-trivial but feasible on an A100. ~3–5× our current step time because of the deeper backward pass.

6. What the X-LoRA paper actually does

Buehler & Buehler (arXiv 2402.07148) train X-LoRA with the next-token-prediction loss — option 4 — for exactly the reason your question raises. Their pitch is: routing is a means, not the target. The thing you actually care about is downstream task performance, so supervise that, and let the routing pattern fall out of optimization.

Their experimental sections never explicitly supervise the gating classifier with cluster labels. The classifier is trained jointly with whatever downstream loss the application requires.

The pooled-CE objective in DESA's notebook 03 (and in our v2) is a weak proxy for that, justified only by the project's tight Colab budget and the convenience of having ED's emotion labels. It is not what the X-LoRA paper does.

7. What this means for our eval results

The X-LoRA v2 classifier learned the cluster signal (pooled accuracy 0.58) but not the per-position routing pattern the architecture is supposed to deliver. At inference, routing weights at each $(t, \ell)$ are slightly tilted toward the gold cluster on average, but each individual decision is close to uniform.

This is "X-LoRA acting like a soft turn-level gate," which is a strict downgrade: it has the cost of token-level routing — extra forward pass for the classifier, FP16 base instead of 4-bit, no native bitsandbytes path — without the benefit of actually routing differently per position.

So in your final eval table, expect:

  • token_level_xlora_v2.gold_cluster_mass: ~0.27–0.32 (better than v1's 0.25 uniform, but much worse than the turn-gate which routes more sharply at the conversation level).
  • mean_entropy: ~1.32–1.38 (close to uniform).
  • PPL: marginally better than uniform_blend, probably worse than turn_level_v2.
The paper-worthy story: "X-LoRA's training objective mismatch — supervising one cluster label while the architecture produces $T \times L$ routing decisions — means the classifier learns the cluster signal at the pooled level but not the per-position sharpness. The simpler turn-level gate, with an aligned objective (one prediction per conversation), actually outperforms X-LoRA in this setup. Recovering X-LoRA's full expressive benefit would require either (a) per-layer or per-position CE, or (b) the original paper's NTP-based training, both of which we leave to future work."

The shortest summary: pooled CE makes per-position routing optional, and gradient descent takes the option to ignore it. To get the per-token magic X-LoRA promises, the supervision granularity has to match the decision granularity — either by supervising at the same granularity (option 2 or 3) or by replacing routing supervision with NTP loss (option 4).
